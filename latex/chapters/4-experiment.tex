\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}


\subsection{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:exp_setup}

We evaluate \textsc{EasyOPD} in three settings that exercise different method-specific interfaces. Cross-tokenizer OPD requires supervision across different teacher and student tokenizations. On-policy self-distillation instantiates teacher and student policies from the same model under different conditioning contexts. Step-wise OPD assigns supervision to intermediate reasoning or tool-interaction steps. Together, these settings cover variation in supervision space, teacher conditioning, and supervision granularity. The three settings are not intended as a single cross-regime leaderboard: they exercise different supervision spaces, teacher-conditioning schemes, and supervision granularities under a shared implementation structure.

\paragraph{Cross-tokenizer OPD.}
Standard OPD directly compares teacher and student next-token distributions, which are not naturally aligned when the two models use different tokenizers. Cross-tokenizer methods address this problem by constructing a shared supervision space. We compare SimCT \citep{sun2026simctrecoveringlostsupervision}, ULD \citep{boizard2025uld}, ALM \citep{minixhofer2025alm}, and DSKD \citep{zhang2024dskd}. We distill Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct \citep{qwen25} into Phi-4-mini-Instruct \citep{phi4mini}. All methods start from the same student checkpoint, warm-started on a shared 10K teacher-generated math and code corpus, and use the same training prompts and optimization budget. We evaluate on MATH500
\citep{lightman2023math500}, GSM8K~\citep{cobbe2021training_gms8k}, MBPP~\citep{austin2021program_mbpp}, and LiveCodeBench v6~\citep{jain2024livecodebench}. We report exact-match accuracy for reasoning, pass@1 for code, and their unweighted mean.

\paragraph{On-policy self-distillation.}
On-policy self-distillation (OPSD) uses the same base model under different conditioning contexts: the student generates a response from the original prompt, while the self-teacher re-evaluates it with additional feedback. We instantiate this setting with SDPO~\citep{hubotter2026sdpo}, using Qwen3-8B~\citep{qwen2025qwen3} as both the student and self-teacher, and compare it with the base model and GRPO~\citep{shao2024deepseekmath}. We evaluate Chemistry from SciKnowEval~\citep{feng2024sciknoweval}, Biology from SciKnowEval~\citep{feng2024sciknoweval}, Tool Use from ToolAlpaca~\citep{tang2023toolalpaca}, and mathematical reasoning on GSM8K~\citep{cobbe2021training_gms8k}. We report the highest Average@16 reached within one-hour and five-hour wall-clock budgets, where Average@16 is the mean correctness over 16 independently sampled responses per question.

\paragraph{Step-wise OPD.}
Response-level OPD~\citep{agarwal2024onpolicy_opsd} applies teacher supervision uniformly across a trajectory, although supervision on later steps may become less reliable after erroneous tool interactions. SOD~\citep{zhong2026sod} instead reweights the distillation loss according to step-level student--teacher divergence. 
We use Qwen3-1.7B~\citep{qwen2025qwen3} as the student and a GRPO-optimized Qwen3-4B~\citep{zhong2026sod} as the teacher. We compare the base model, GRPO~\citep{shao2024deepseekmath}, response-level OPD~\citep{agarwal2024onpolicy_opsd}, and SOD~\citep{zhong2026sod}. All methods use the same SFT initialization, training data, and optimization budget \citep{zhong2026sod}. We evaluate on AIME 2024, AIME 2025~\citep{maa_aime}, and LiveCodeBench v6~\citep{jain2024livecodebench}. Each benchmark score is Average@32 over 32 independently sampled responses per problem, and Avg.\ is the unweighted mean across the three benchmarks.

\paragraph{Shared implementation.}
All methods are implemented in \textsc{EasyOPD} and executed through the same verl-based infrastructure~\citep{sheng2024hybridflow_verl}. Within each setting, we keep the model configuration, training data, execution backend, and evaluation procedure fixed where applicable, while retaining each method's original supervision design. Additional optimization, sampling, hardware, and checkpoint details are provided in the Appendix~\ref{app:exp_setup}.


\subsection{Results}
\label{sec:exp_results}

\paragraph{Cross-tokenizer OPD.}
Table~\ref{tab:cross_tokenizer_results} shows that all four evaluated cross-tokenizer methods improve the unweighted average over the base student in this setting. SimCT obtains the highest observed average of 53.5, 3.3 points above the base model, and the highest MATH500 score. ALM obtains the highest scores on MBPP and LiveCodeBench v6, whereas DSKD performs best on GSM8K. These results verify that the evaluated cross-tokenizer objectives can be executed through \textsc{EasyOPD}'s alignment and loss interfaces under the matched setup, while the methods retain distinct task-dependent performance profiles.

\paragraph{On-policy self-distillation.}
Table~\ref{tab:self_opd_results} compares SDPO and GRPO in the OPSD evaluation setting under the two wall-clock budgets. At one hour, SDPO is 19.2 points higher than GRPO on both Chemistry and Biology. At five hours, the corresponding differences are 6.7 and 6.0 points. SDPO and GRPO obtain numerically similar one-hour results on Tool Use, while GRPO is higher after five hours. GRPO also obtains higher GSM8K scores under both budgets. SDPO's relative performance is therefore task-dependent in the evaluated setting: its largest gains occur on Chemistry and Biology, while GRPO is stronger on GSM8K and on the five-hour Tool Use result.

\paragraph{Step-wise OPD.}
Table~\ref{tab:stepwise_opd_results} shows that response-level OPD exceeds GRPO by 4.07, 9.79, and 8.34 points on AIME 2024, AIME 2025, and LiveCodeBench v6, respectively. SOD obtains 43.65, 38.33, and 37.88 on the three benchmarks, for an unweighted average of 39.95, the highest observed score on all three benchmarks. Relative to response-level OPD, the corresponding gains are 5.52, 2.29, and 7.61 points. Under this setup, the results are consistent with the intended role of step-wise weighting; they do not by themselves establish that every step-wise OPD method will outperform response-level OPD.

\paragraph{Overall findings.}
Across the three case studies, \textsc{EasyOPD} executes the evaluated methods through the same verl-based backend while preserving their distinct alignment, teacher-conditioning, and supervision-granularity requirements. The results should be interpreted within each setting rather than as a cross-regime ranking.

\subsection{Extensibility Analysis}
\label{sec:exp_extensibility}

To characterize integration cost, we compare an alignment-heavy method (SimCT) and a step-heavy method (SOD). Table~\ref{tab:extensibility} reports, for each method, the number of shared dispatch sites it touches in the verl core, the method-local files, the method-specific lines of code, and the configuration size. All counts are taken from the released repository. The shared distillation hook layer is introduced once in a small, fixed set of core files (\texttt{verl/trainer/distillation/}, and the dispatch points in \texttt{ray\_trainer.py} and \texttt{dp\_actor.py}); each evaluated method then registers its loss and, where needed, adds a routing entry at these existing dispatch sites, while its supervision logic lives entirely in method-local modules.

For the evaluated methods, supervision logic is placed in three method-local files each (\texttt{\_\_init\_\_.py}, a hooks module, and a losses or core module), amounting to roughly 868 and 438 lines for SimCT and SOD, respectively, with 57 and 64 configuration lines. SimCT exercises cross-tokenizer alignment, the reused teacher sidecar, and the loss interface, while SOD exercises rollout metadata, the teacher sidecar, and the loss interface for step-level weighting. Adding a method thus concentrates almost all new code in method-local modules and reuses the existing shared dispatch layer rather than introducing new method-specific components across the core trainer and workers.
