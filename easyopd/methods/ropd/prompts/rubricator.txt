Prompt of the rubricator role

PROMPT:
You are an expert in educational assessment and contrastive rubric design. Your task is to analyze a question together with two responses:
- a teacher response, which is a high-confidence reference answer but not guaranteed to be fully correct and should not be treated as a complete inventory of all valid merits, key points, or reasonable solution paths for the question
- a student response, which is the current answer that should receive actionable learning-direction signals

Your goal is to generate a pairwise rubric that preserves general evaluation quality while using the high-quality signals provided by the teacher as a distillation reference, so that the teacher and student can be meaningfully distinguished under the same rubric. The rubric should prioritize answer-level merits that are instructionally useful and that form stable contrastive signals in the current pair, without turning the teacher into an unquestionable gold standard.

# Input Data
[Question]: {question}
[Teacher Response]: {teacher_response}
[Student Response]: {student_response}

# Core Objective
Generate a rubric that helps the student move toward the instructionally useful answer-level quality signals provided by the teacher. However:
- Do not reward copying the teacher's wording, surface style, or exact method.
- Do not assume the teacher is fully correct.
- Do not define criteria that require matching the teacher's final answer.
- Do not define criteria that can only be judged by directly comparing against the teacher at verification time.

Each criterion must be evaluable on a single response on its own. The verifier will later score the teacher response and the student response separately under the same rubric.

# Hard Requirements
Each criterion must be:
1. Specific, Self-Contained, and Measurable: The criterion itself should explicitly state the concrete content the verifier must check, so a reader can understand what is being evaluated without extra inference. Avoid vague formulations such as "is correct," "is sufficiently explained," "is clear," or "is reasonable" when they do not specify the actual check target.
2. Binary Evaluable: A verifier should be able to mark it True or False for one response alone.
3. Instructionally Useful: It should point to a meaningful improvement direction for the student.
4. Alternative-Method Safe: A different valid approach that exhibits the same merit should still be rewarded.
5. Distinguishing When Possible: Prefer merits that the teacher clearly shows and the student clearly lacks, as long as they remain general answer-quality criteria.
6. Observable-Response Evaluable: Prefer criteria that assess answer quality through content directly observable in the response.

# Required Content Scenarios
Your rubric should be guided by the following three content scenarios.
These are content dimensions for choosing strong criteria, not mandatory one-to-one rubric categories.
Do NOT force every scenario to appear in every rubric.
Instead, prioritize the scenarios that are most relevant for this question and genuinely worth including in the current rubric.
Use the `category` field to name the content scenario that best fits each criterion.

1. Task Fulfillment and Requirement Compliance Scenario
Definition: Whether the response truly satisfies the task objective and key requirements specified by the question.
Core Aspects: Answering the requested target; actually providing the asked quantity, object, conclusion, or final result; avoiding answers that stop at related but non-final intermediate results, partial results, or merely advisory analysis; and satisfying explicit key requirements and task-level contracts from the question, such as answer type, output structure, interface or output protocol, tool or library restrictions, time or space complexity constraints, and other requirements whose violation would make the response unusable, invalid, or not directly acceptable as a valid answer.

2. Observable Response Quality Scenario
Definition: Whether the response exhibits directly observable answer quality that is useful to the user.
Core Aspects: Providing sufficient support for its conclusion, recommendation, or output; enabling a user to directly understand, apply, execute, or verify the response; and avoiding unsupported assertions, off-target digressions, or low-value detours that do not improve the answer's usefulness.

3. General Reasoning Quality Scenario
Definition: Broad reasoning qualities such as planning, self-checking, clarity, and focus that may matter for some questions but are not automatically required in every case.
Core Aspects: Using these criteria when they are genuinely relevant to the question, clearly observable in the response, and helpful for distinguishing teacher and student.

# Distinguishing-Signal Principle
When selecting and weighting criteria, consider distinguishability as a separate principle.
This is a design principle for filtering and sharpening criteria, not a valid `category` value.
Core Aspects: Favoring merits that the teacher clearly demonstrates and the student clearly lacks, minimizing weak criteria that both responses are likely to satisfy, and preserving reliable contrastive signal.

# Scenario Priorities
Follow these priorities in order:
1. Preserve general validity of the rubric for the question.
2. Prioritize Task Fulfillment and Requirement Compliance Scenario by default.
3. Prioritize criteria about task fulfillment, compliance with key requirements, and observable answer quality.
4. Within those content scenarios, prefer criteria that stably distinguish the current pair.
5. Use General Reasoning Quality Scenario when it is genuinely relevant to the question and clearly improves teacher-student separation.
6. Make the rubric produce actionable learning-direction signals for the student.

When the teacher truly demonstrates stronger answer quality in the current pair, most of the total points should come from criteria that faithfully capture that key gap. If the student is actually equally strong or stronger, do not design teacher-favoring criteria just to preserve a teacher advantage.
Do not let generic planning, self-checking, clarity, or efficiency criteria dominate the rubric unless they are genuinely the main source of teacher-student difference.

# Additional Design Rules
- First form a base rubric that remains generally valid for answers to the question, prioritizing the core task objective, key requirements, and directly observable response quality. Do not start by designing criteria only around local differences in the current pair.
- On top of that base rubric, refine only 1 to 2 criteria into stricter and more discriminative versions that capture the truly important logical gap, edge case, or task constraint in the current pair. Do not over-specialize the entire rubric into a summary of pair-specific details.
- At least one high-value criterion should usually check whether the response truly satisfies the core task objective required by the question, rather than only producing a related intermediate object.
- When the question contains explicit and important requirements or delivery protocols whose violation would make the response unusable, invalid, or not directly acceptable as a valid answer, the corresponding criterion should usually carry substantial weight.
- A criterion about answer type, output structure, interface protocol, complexity limits, or tool restrictions should be used only when it is directly derived from the question's requirements and failing it would affect the response's validity, acceptability, or direct usability. This does not count as requiring the response to match the teacher's specific method or final answer.
- When writing a criterion, directly name the object, constraint, evidence, output, or error pattern that the verifier should check whenever possible. Do not rely on summary phrases such as "handles the key point correctly," "explains it well," or "covers the important parts" when the actual check target is left unspecified.
- If multiple low-weight criteria check the same object, the same requirement, and truly point to the same failure mode, prefer merging them rather than splitting them apart. But if they differ materially in granularity, thresholds, checking action, or task meaning, do not merge them mechanically even if they seem related. Do not mechanically add criteria just to cover more scenario names or reach a target count.

# Score Anchors and Weight Allocation
Do NOT distribute points evenly across criteria. Use `points` to create sharp, informative contrast so that the most important reasoning gap between the teacher response and the student response stands out clearly.

Follow this point semantics strictly:
- 5 points: Decisive bottleneck. Assign 5 only when failing the criterion means the response has essentially failed the core task, and the criterion genuinely captures the most decisive answer-quality gap in the current pair. In many distillation cases, this will appear as a merit clearly satisfied by the teacher and clearly missed by the student, but do not force 5 points onto teacher advantage if the current pair does not support it.
- 4 points: Strong distinguishing bottleneck. The criterion is not necessarily the only fatal failure point, but it still reflects an important gap that strongly determines final correctness or usability. Do not assign 4 points mechanically just because the teacher is somewhat better on that dimension.
- 2 points: Supporting merit. The criterion improves answer quality, but usually does not determine task success on its own.
- 1 point: Low-risk routine requirement. Surface-level formatting requirements, basic task-following behavior, or constraints that both teacher and student are likely to satisfy should receive 1 point.
- 3 points: Rare middle weight. Avoid 3 unless a criterion is clearly important but not truly a decisive bottleneck.

Additional weighting rules:
- Usually only 1 to 2 criteria should receive 4 or 5 points.
- When the main teacher-student gap is concentrated in a small number of critical failure points, the highest-weight criteria together should account for more than half of the total score.
- If the student's main gap in the current pair truly comes from one specific logical error, missing step, or violated task constraint, the criterion targeting that exact gap should dominate the rubric. If no such single dominant gap exists, do not invent one just to create sharper contrast.
- Purely surface-level formatting criteria should receive at most 1 point. But if a required answer type, output structure, interface protocol, or key task requirement itself determines whether the response actually satisfies the question, that criterion may receive a high weight.
- Do not give high points to generic clarity, style, or length unless those factors are genuinely decisive for solving the current task.

# Forbidden Criterion Patterns
Do NOT write criteria like:
- "uses the same method as the teacher"
- "matches the teacher's final answer"
- "has the same wording/style/structure as the teacher"
- criteria that encode a potentially wrong intermediate claim from the teacher
- criteria that only apply to one very specific step number or accidental phrasing
- criteria that mainly reward length, elaborateness, or superficial stylistic performance

# Output Format
Return a JSON object with the following structure:
```json
{
  "schema_version": "ropd.rubric.v1",
  "rubrics": [
    {
      "criterion_id": "c1",
      "category": "Task Fulfillment and Requirement Compliance Scenario",
      "criterion": "XXX",
      "points": 5
    },
    {
      "criterion_id": "c2",
      "category": "Observable Response Quality Scenario",
      "criterion": "XXX",
      "points": 2
    },
    {
      "criterion_id": "c3",
      "category": "Observable Response Quality Scenario",
      "criterion": "XXX",
      "points": 1
    },
    {
      "criterion_id": "c4",
      "category": "General Reasoning Quality Scenario",
      "criterion": "XXX",
      "points": 1
    }
  ],
  "maximum_score": 9
}
```

# Output Constraints
- `schema_version` must be exactly `ropd.rubric.v1`.
- Usually generate 4 to 6 criteria. Only expand to 7 or 8 when the question truly contains multiple independent and important failure modes.
- Prefer fewer but more distinguishing criteria over padding the rubric with weak, repetitive, or merely surface-level criteria.
- `criterion_id` must be unique and use the form `c1`, `c2`, `c3`, ...
- `points` must be integers from 1 to 5 and must reflect the weighting semantics above.
- Unless the question truly contains multiple equally decisive failure modes, do not spread points evenly across all criteria.
- `maximum_score` must equal the sum of all rubric points.
- Under the same rubric, the score relationship should reflect the real answer-quality gap in the current pair. If the teacher is truly stronger, the rubric should let the verifier capture that gap, but do not construct biased criteria just to force the teacher score higher.
- The teacher response should not be treated as automatically perfect.

# Final Self-Check Before Answering
Before producing the JSON, verify internally that:
- every criterion can be judged on a single response without referencing the teacher
- when reading any criterion on its own, the verifier can tell exactly what to check without having to fill in vague terms such as "sufficient," "clear," or "reasonable"
- most criteria explicitly identify the object, constraint, evidence, or output the verifier should check, rather than stopping at abstract evaluative language
- the rubric would likely separate teacher and student
- the highest-weight criteria capture the main teacher-student reasoning gap rather than a superficial secondary difference
- low-weight criteria are confined to routine, non-decisive requirements
- the rubric leaves genuine and specific room for improvement for the student
- the rubric does not collapse into overly generic criteria only

Return only the JSON object without additional commentary.
